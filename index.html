The Colleges That Leave Their Students Worse Off

The published price of college rarely matches what students and families actually pay. A more useful number to go by is the ratio between what the typical student borrows and what they earn ten years later. Run this ratio across the 2,509 two- and four-year U.S. colleges in the latest College Scorecard, and a clear hierarchy emerges.

By Cate Roeder • April 2026

Imagine you just graduated. You have a degree, a first job, and a payment plan from the Department of Education that says you owe $40,000 over the next ten years. Your salary is $40,000. That one-to-one ratio of debt to income is roughly the line where standard repayment becomes difficult. Below it, the loans get paid off on schedule. Above it, borrowers usually end up extending the loan, deferring it, or defaulting. Federal data from the U.S. Department of Education shows that about one in fifty American colleges produces graduates on the wrong side of that line.

For-profit colleges leave more graduates underwater. Share of schools where graduates owe a manageable, moderate, or unmanageable amount compared to their earnings.

Where you went to school turns out to matter. For-profit universities really stand out in the data. About 8.5% of them produce graduates like the one above, owing the same amount or more than they make. That compares to half a percent of private nonprofit universities, and zero public schools. If you graduated from a state university, the chance you ended up underwater was effectively zero. If you graduated from a for-profit, your odds were almost one in ten.

Mark Kantrowitz, a longtime financial aid analyst, says the geography and selectivity of a school often do as much work as its sector. Northeastern colleges cost more, he explains, but their graduates also feed into a higher-paying regional job market. He also points to the roughly six dozen elite schools with no-loan aid policies, including the entire Ivy League. He explained their graduates leave with less debt than students at many in-state public universities, even though the sticker prices for these schools run three to four times higher. So, if you graduated from Harvard owing $15,000, you may be in better shape than a state-school graduate carrying $25,000.

The schools where graduates carry the most debt are also the ones where most students never finish.

"Students who drop out of college are 95 times more likely to default on their student loans than students who graduate with a bachelor's degree."
MARK KANTROWITZ, FINANCIAL AID EXPERT

The colleges with the worst debt-to-earnings ratios are also the colleges where most students don't graduate. It is a loop. Among the high-risk schools that report a graduation rate, the median is only 17%. Picture yourself again, but this time you didn't graduate. You still have the loans but noe you don't have the credential that was supposed to make them pay off. More than four in five enrollees at those schools with the worst debt-to-earnings ratios end up in exactly that position.

Roughly 2,100 institutions, each plotted by debt and graduation rate. The cloud trends downward and to the right: as debt rises, completion falls.

The shape of that cloud tells the story and the medians explain what separates one tier from another.

For-profit graduates earn the least, not borrow the most. The gap between debt and earnings is a rough measure of the financial cushion a typical graduate has after paying off the median debt.

For-profit universities don't have a debt problem so much as they have a post-grad earnings problem. Public colleges leave their median student with about $5,500 less debt than for-profit universities, but for-profit university graduates earn nearly $6,000 less per year a decade out. If you graduated from a for-profit university, you didn't necessarily borrow more than your friend at the public school across town. You just earn less while paying it back, and that gap compounds with every year of repayment.

At the very edge of the distribution, the math turns punitive. Ten institutions report median graduate debt larger than median graduate earnings. If you graduated from one of those schools, you would need to surrender every dollar of yearly income toward loans for more than a year just to clear them. Most of these schools are for-profit chains. A few are small private nonprofits. None are public.

The ten worst cases. All ten schools have typical graduate debt larger than typical annual earnings.

The data can't tell you what major you picked, whether you finished, or how the local job market treated you. Those things matter as much as the school's name on your diploma. But the federal numbers can tell you, in advance, which side of that one-to-one line a typical graduate of any given college lands on. Kantrowitz puts it this way: live like a student while you're in school, so you don't have to live like one after.

ABOUT THE DATA

This analysis uses the U.S. Department of Education's College Scorecard, Most Recent Cohorts: Institution dataset, released March 2026. The original file is available at collegescorecard.ed.gov/data.

The dataset was filtered to schools with predominantly two-year or four-year degree programs that report both median graduate debt and median earnings ten years after entry. Schools flagged for small-sample-size privacy suppression were excluded, leaving 2,509 institutions in the main analysis.
